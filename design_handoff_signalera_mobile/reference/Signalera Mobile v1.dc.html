Turn 3 · where this is over-built

Three tabs, because the tabs should be the tiers.

the honest answer

Not too complex. Wrong in one place.

The core loop is at the sweet spot and I would not touch it. Today, the commit gesture, the closing screen, the record and its four states are the right amount of thing. The density inside a card is also right: an analyst deciding whether to put their name on a claim needs the whole claim, and a lighter version would be a worse product, not a simpler one.

The wrong place is Markets. I criticised Radar for being a container named after a container, then built one. Markets holds a news feed, a deal database, a company search, a theme list and a chat. The only thing those five share is that I did not know where else to put them. That is a junk drawer, and a junk drawer is the one structure that gets worse every time the product grows.

The fix is not a better folder, it is dissolving it. Live Feed defaults to your followed names, which is Watch. Trends is themes you follow, which is Watch. Deal Flow is deals you follow, which is Watch. Company Intel is search, and search is a field, not a destination. Intelligence is a conversation about your own material, so it should be reachable from any screen rather than parked at the end of a list.

What is left is three tabs that are the three engagement tiers, in order of commitment. Today is where claims are offered. Record is tier three, the things you committed to. Watch is tiers one and two, the things you follow and the things you have a view on but will not stake. The navigation stops being a menu and starts being the product's own model of engagement. Search and Intelligence sit in the header, available everywhere.

What it costs, plainly. Deal Flow loses its own front door, so a user who wants to survey every deal now filters inside Watch rather than landing on a deal screen. For a private equity recruiting audience that is a real loss and it is the strongest argument for keeping four tabs. Company Intel behind search means you cannot browse it, only ask for a name. And Watch inherits the risk it just solved: if everything you follow lands there, it has to stay one list with one membership rule, or it becomes the next junk drawer.

Under-built, separately. First run hands the user a three-tier legend before they have seen a single call. Nobody reads a manual. The tiers should be taught by using them: the first time a card is followed, one line explains what following is, and never again. That is the next thing I would build.

Turn 2 · the Dashboard question, and the five reference surfaces

Everything I demoted, built out.

Two answers to the Dashboard question and full screens for all five Markets surfaces. Each one is built from the live page's real structure, read out of the repo this morning: Live Feed's buckets and pills, Deal Flow's four stages, Company Intel's tabs and range chips, Trends' date grouping, and Intelligence rebuilt so it cannot become a scoreboard.

the brainstorm, before the two screens

Three shapes a mobile Dashboard could take

One, the widget stack. Port the desktop widgets down a column: market cards, lead story, The Watch, Your calls, Watchlist, Following, Daily Briefs. Honest about parity and genuinely bad on a phone. Seven widgets at 390px is roughly 3,400px of scroll before you have made a single decision, every widget is a worse version of the screen it links to, and the two things that matter, the brief and the record, are the fourth and fifth things you meet. I did not build this one because building it is the argument against it.

Two, the delta screen. Stop aggregating and start differencing. A dashboard on a phone has exactly one job the other screens cannot do: tell you what changed since you last looked. So it opens with the timestamp of your last visit and lists only what moved since, four rows, each one a doorway. Nothing is duplicated, nothing is summarised, and it is short enough to read standing up. This is 2a.

Three, the delta as a block inside Today. Same content as two, but it is not a tab. It is a collapsed row at the top of Today that expands into the same four items, and it collapses itself once read. You keep four tabs, the day still opens on a decision, and the catch-up is one tap away rather than in the way. This is 2a2.

What the fifth tab actually costs. Not width. At 390px five tabs are 78px each and the labels still fit. It costs primacy: whatever sits in position one is what the product says it is for, and a menu in position one says this is a product you browse. It also splits the morning into two screens that both claim to be the start of the day, which is the one ambiguity a ritual cannot survive. If you want the fifth tab anyway, 2a is a good screen and I would ship it. I just would not put it first.

What I did while you were asleep

1 · Recon, and what the capture could not reach

The product. Signalera publishes a daily market brief containing falsifiable calls, each carrying a direction and a date it gets checked. A user adopts one with a single gesture, that adoption fixes a window that cannot be moved afterwards, and when the window closes the claim is checked against real prices using benchmark attribution: a move the market or the sector explains is not evidence. What accumulates is a timestamped record of how the user thought, misses included. That record is the product. Everything else is input to it.

Reached by live capture. signalera.ai marketing shell, masthead and type treatment, the Heritage Gold on cream palette, the Playfair and Inter and JetBrains Mono triad, the vocabulary set supported / challenged / developing / awaiting, and the positioning line about tracking which calls are supported rather than summarising the market.

Not reached, authentication required. Every logged-in surface. Dashboard, Morning Brief, Evening Wrap, Live Feed, Radar and its three tabs, Deal Flow, Company Intel, Trends, Intelligence. Those came from your four screenshots, which cover Dashboard only, at desktop width. I therefore have direct visual reference for exactly one of the nine surfaces.

Designed fresh, stated plainly. Morning Brief, Evening Wrap, Radar > Calls, Watch, and the Markets index are designed from the repo's data model and copy rather than recreated from a screen I have seen. I have not reconstructed a layout I could not look at. Deal Flow, Company Intel, Trends and Intelligence are represented only as entry rows, because designing their interiors from nothing would be invention.

Read in the repo (structure and vocabulary only, not run): src/components/calls/TrackCallControl.tsx, src/lib/call-horizons.ts, src/lib/desk-record.ts, src/components/record/DeskRecordView.tsx, src/components/scored-object/ScoredObject.tsx, src/components/thesis/OutcomeBadge.tsx, src/components/calls/HorizonChip.tsx, src/components/shell/mobile-bottom-nav.tsx. Every ledger line, horizon phrase and terms sentence in the prototype is lifted from those files rather than written by me.

Three contradictions, flagged rather than silently resolved

One. Your screenshot of the live dashboard shows a graded record block reading 60% with a W and L split, and individual entries labelled with the word the vocabulary rule forbids, plus one labelled ungradable. Your brief says no aggregate accuracy figure may exist anywhere and names the four permitted outcome words. These cannot both be true. I built to the brief and reproduced none of it. Shipping the brief's rule means deleting that block from the live dashboard.

Two. The marketing site carries a percentage of evidence supported and a count of supported checks. Same conflict, same resolution: absent from everything I built.

Three. The repo's grading model has five outcomes, not four. Alongside supported and challenged it has a distinct state for a move that could not be separated from its sector or the market, which the code calls no clean read, and a separate state for an absence of any grade. Your four words have nowhere to put a confounded read. I mapped confounded to developing, kept the attribution sentence underneath so the distinction survives in the evidence line, and mapped ungraded to awaiting. This is the single decision in the whole exercise most likely to be wrong, because it makes a confounded read look like a live one.

2 · The principles I designed against, and where each comes from

A ritual needs an end, not just a beginning. Products people genuinely open every morning almost always have a terminal state: a finite set that runs out, a page that says you are done. The pattern is common to the daily crossword, the daily language lesson, the daily news digest that ends. Feeds never end and therefore never satisfy. Signalera's brief is naturally finite, five or six calls, and the current product renders it as a page you scroll rather than a set you complete. Making it a set that closes is the highest-leverage structural change available, and it costs nothing in content.

Commitment needs proportionate physical friction. Where an action carries consequence, good products ask for a gesture whose effort matches the stakes: press and keep pressing, drag to a target, type a word. A dialog asking are you sure is not friction, it is an obstacle, and users learn to dismiss it without reading. Track this call fixes a window that cannot be moved and puts a miss permanently on a record. A tap is too cheap for that. Seven hundred milliseconds of deliberate pressure is not.

Progressive disclosure should go from claim to reasoning, never from summary to detail. Reading apps and long-form readers get this right: the first thing you see is the actual first sentence, not a description of it. Applied here, the skimmable layer is the claim itself in full, the direction and the date, because those are what a user decides on. The desk's reasoning expands beneath. Nothing is summarised into a shorter version of itself.

A longitudinal record earns trust by being uncurated and hard to reorder. Journals, lab notebooks and version histories are credible because entries sit where they fell and cannot be rearranged. The moment a record can be sorted best first it stops being evidence. The record here is reverse chronological, unfiltered by default, with challenged entries at identical size and weight, and the filter chips carry counts rather than any derived figure. This principle is already in the repo's own model, which refuses to filter and refuses a hero number. I did not invent it, I moved it to the home tab.

Dense hierarchy on a small viewport is solved by one object repeated, not by many layouts. The most information-dense mobile surfaces that stay legible use a single card anatomy everywhere and vary only its state. One anatomy here: state edge, entity, claim, evidence, ledger. It appears in the brief, in the wrap, in the record and in the compact record. A user learns to read it once.

Thumb reachability is a hierarchy statement, not an ergonomics footnote. The bottom third is where a one-handed thumb lives. Whatever is there is what the product thinks you came for. Every commit control in this design lives in a sheet that rises into that band, and every navigation pole is a bottom tab. The top of the screen carries only things you read.

A limitation, stated. These are written from what I know of the pattern space rather than from a fresh literature review, and the two that matter most, the completion state and the pressure gesture, are the ones I would want tested first with real students on a real commute.

3 · The return problem, without a push

Push is unavailable in practice, so the product has to be pulled open. Four mechanisms, in order of how much work each does.

The open window is the trigger. Every committed call has a fixed date on which it is checked, chosen by the system and stated at commit time. The user leaves the app owed an answer. That is a durable obligation the product did not have to manufacture, and unlike a streak it cannot be broken by missing a day, only satisfied. This is why the record is a top-level pole and not a tab inside Radar: the thing pulling them back has to be one tap from the icon.

The first two seconds pay the obligation, not a greeting. Screen 02 leads with how many of the user's own calls are reviewed today and names them. On a quiet day it says nothing of yours is reviewed today, names the nearest date, and moves on. Compare the live dashboard, which opens with a personalised greeting and a profile completion prompt. A greeting is not a reason to have unlocked the phone.

The day states its own next event. The close screen ends with the evening wrap time, and the wrap ends with tomorrow's macro print and the brief time. The user always leaves knowing when the next thing exists. That is a scheduled appointment the user keeps themselves, which is what a habit is.

The icon and the install are a designed moment. The prompt fires once, only after a first commit and only on a second visit, and it argues from the user's own open window rather than from our convenience. The prompt carries the current wordmark, rendered from src/components/ui/wordmark.tsx rather than redrawn: the word set in Playfair with "era" filled by a vertical gold gradient. The home screen tile is the wordmark's own S in the same face and the same gold, because a 60px square cannot hold nine letters and a crop of the logo is more honest than a second mark. Both are at 13b. Declined once means never asked again.

What I deliberately did not do: no streak, no completion percentage, no badge, no count of consecutive mornings, no daily goal, and no notification-shaped element wearing a different name. The obligation is to a claim the user made, not to us.

4 · The three directions, scored

Full write-ups of the two that died are at 1b and 1c. Against the eight tests you set: The Wire fails on ritual completion, on tier distinction and on how a challenged entry survives a scroll. The Desk fails on burying the core loop three taps deep and on having no point of view, and it is the responsive squeeze in polite clothing. The Ledger fails nothing outright, but it is the only one of the three that requires you to defend demoting four surfaces, and it puts more weight on a completion state than the current content pipeline may reliably fill. The Wire's one good idea, that resolutions and new calls belong in the same stream, survives inside the Evening Wrap. The Desk's one good idea, that the two briefs are distinct destinations, survives as the two halves of Today.

In plain terms: the record is the product, so the record is the home. A structure where the heart of the product is a tab inside a container named Radar is a structure that does not believe its own positioning.

6 · Where I expect you to push back

Dashboard is gone. It is your most-visited screen and I dissolved it. My reasoning: at 390px an aggregation screen is a worse copy of everything it aggregates, and its only unique job is telling you what changed while you were away, which Today's first two lines do better because they say it about your own claims. If you disagree, the cheapest reversal is a fifth tab, and the design survives it.

The word Radar does not survive. It is brand vocabulary and I traded it for a word that says what the screen contains. If Radar is load-bearing for the brand this is a rename, not a restructure, and costs nothing to undo.

Press and keep will read as friction to some users. That is the intent, and it is also the risk: it is unfamiliar, it is not discoverable without the label, and on a moving train a seven hundred millisecond press is genuinely harder than a tap. The fallback is a two tap sheet with the same copy. I would test the pressure version first because the gesture is the only part of the product that physically feels like putting your name on something.

The completion screen assumes a finite brief. If the pipeline sometimes emits eleven calls, or two, the ritual loses its shape. I would cap the decidable set at six and let the rest live as reading, but that is a content decision and it is yours.

Counts in the record. Defended above. If your counsel reads a bucket count as an implied rate, delete the numbers from the chips and the design is unharmed.

Minimalist, confirmed with one correction. You asked me to check your guy's instinct. He is right about the surface and wrong about the density. Reducing the number of things on screen is correct, because a commute affords one decision at a time. Reducing the amount of substance inside each thing is not: an analyst reading a claim needs the whole claim, the attribution figures and the date, and a version that shows only a headline and a pill reads as a consumer app and destroys the thing the record is for. So: few objects, one anatomy, generous space around them, and full editorial weight inside them. Restraint in the layout, not in the content. That is also the only way this stays recognisably Signalera rather than becoming a well-behaved finance app skin.

7 · Self audit, what I found and fixed

Vocabulary scan
Scanned every string for the six banned words as substrings, not just as words, which is the scan that matters: the violations were all inside longer words. Six real hits, all fixed. A take-private written with the word containing b-u-y. A homebuilder claim built on rate incentives, originally written with the word containing b-u-y. A payments claim naming merchants, originally written with the word containing s-e-l-l. A sector claim using the word containing p-e-r-f-o-r-m. Two prose uses of the verb containing h-o-l-d. And, most importantly, the commit button itself, which read Hold to commit and now reads Press to commit, then Keep pressing while pressed. That last one would have shipped a banned word onto the most consequential control in the product. Outcome words are the four permitted ones only. No instance of right, wrong, correct, win or loss. No em-dashes anywhere.
Aggregate figures
Zero. No percentage of anything derived from the record, no ratio, no total described as a score. The only percentages on screen are price moves attached to a named instrument over a named window.
Contrast
Measured rather than estimated, and it took three passes. Espresso on cream is 18.2:1 and body copy 13.2:1. The two supporting tones failed the first pass at 4.11:1 and 2.40:1, and the second pass fixed them against cream only, which missed that the metadata tone sits on parchment wells and chat inputs where it measured 4.25. Both are now tuned against the darkest surface they touch: 5.9:1 and 4.9:1, passing on every background in the design. The active navigation label was gold on cream at 2.2:1, on nineteen screens, on the primary wayfinding signal; it is espresso now and gold survives as the icon and the rule. The rule I should have written first, and which the third pass applies everywhere: below 14px, text is espresso or the metadata neutral, and colour carries meaning only as a fill, a border, a dot, a rule or an icon. That is why the deal stage badge reads with an espresso word and a coloured dot rather than a coloured word at 2.15:1, why the active range chip states itself with a fill and a border rather than a gold label, and why no gold appears on any text at any size. Signal green moved one step darker to 4.9:1 and challenged red reads 8.2:1. No state is signalled by colour alone: every one carries a word, and now every word is legible.
Tap targets
Every control is at least 44px tall, but only after a second pass caught sixty-two that were not. The pattern was consistent and wrong: chips, range keys, segmented controls and tab pills were authored at 34 to 42px with no padding, so the stated minimum was also the finished height. All are now 44. Tab bar rows are 56 and the commit control is 54. The only elements still under 44 are the deal stage badges at 22px, which are labels and not targets.
Chrome collision
Screen 14 measures both states. Content is 610px with chrome and 684px standalone. No control of ours enters the bottom 50px band Safari owns, and the tab bar sits above it rather than behind it. Every height is dvh based, so the address bar collapsing on scroll changes available space without moving anything.
Still open
Three things. The compact record row relies on a two line clamp, so an unusually long claim truncates without a way to see the rest in place; it needs a tap to open, which exists, but the truncation is a small dishonesty on a screen whose whole point is hiding nothing. Second, the gold link tone measures 3.3:1 on cream, so it is sound for the large and the decorative but not for small text; it survives only on arrow glyphs that sit beside a labelled control. Third, and worth saying plainly: two rounds of this audit asserted numbers the screens did not meet. The lesson I am taking is that a self audit written from intent rather than from measurement is worth nothing, and that section is now written from measurement.
